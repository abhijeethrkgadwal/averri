# Build vs Pass debate

## Advocate position: build-narrow

Build it, but build the narrow thing the research actually supports, and treat the first six months as a priced experiment with a pre-registered exit rather than the first six months of a company.

**Why this is a build and not another pivot.** The founder's question is whether to keep pivoting until a space appears. The evidence says the space has already appeared and it is unusually well-measured for a pre-product idea. Veracode 2026 and the CSA note show 45-70% of agent-written samples fail security tests, with missing authorization the dominant class, and that rate has been flat across model generations while syntax quality went to near-perfect. Supabase, Lovable (170/1,645 showcase apps with RLS off), Moltbook (1.5M tokens exposed), and Sygnia's pentest are the incident log. GitLab's 1,528-respondent survey has 84% saying the hard part is governing code after creation and 85% saying the bottleneck moved to review. Google, Anthropic, and DX put agent-authored share at 50-80% and rising. That is a demand curve going up on volume and flat on quality. Pivoting away from a measured, growing failure mode toward an unmeasured idea is the actual failure pattern; this idea has already survived a research round, three proposals, and critics, and has converged on exactly the shape the 2030 analysis says wins in the medium scenario (50%): TypeScript-embedded declarations compiled to a graph, attached to existing stacks, no new syntax.

**The one honest reframe: sell the gate, not the bot.** Every brief agrees the review layer is crowded and bundled at zero marginal cost (CodeRabbit, Cursor/Graphite, Greptile TREX, Copilot, Claude Code Review, Codex, Devin). Warrant should not enter that market. The future-market brief puts a ~60% chance that a platform ships something capturing most of the Team-tier PR-bot value within 24 months, but only ~25% that anyone eliminates the enterprise policy-gate value, because a neutral, on-prem, cross-agent, merge-blocking gate is structurally unattractive to every vendor that wants its own agent to be the trusted one. GitHub's own review still cannot block. So the narrowed product is: the Apache-2.0 declaration library plus `suggest` (agents draft the contracts, humans approve spec cards in a PR), the deterministic auth-matrix and pitfall test generation, and a required-check gate ("no PR may weaken a `requires` or remove a `policy` on an entity tagged payment") sold to regulated TypeScript SaaS teams. The $20/dev bot becomes the free-to-cheap adoption surface, not the revenue line. This also answers the competition brief's sharpest point: incumbents infer intent from diffs and prose rules, and CodeRabbit has no incentive to ask developers to write declarations because its whole model is zero-config. Warrant's bet is that agents will do the declaring now; `suggest` is that bet made concrete, and it is the only reason the declared-contract layer being "empty" (Oso to Tiny, Wasp dropping its DSL) is not simply a graveyard.

**Revenue realism.** Concede the plan's numbers. $150-250K ARR at M12 is top-decile for a two-person open-core devtool; CodeRabbit, with a two-minute install and a Series A, took 18 months to $1M, and Inngest took three years. The revenue brief's base case ($0-60K at M12, $100-250K at M18, $300K-1M at M36) should be the plan of record. But the decision in front of the founder is not "will this reach $500K ARR by M18"; it is "is $150-200K and six months a fair price for a decision-grade answer." It is, for three reasons. First, the benchmark gate is pre-registered with explicit kill criteria (B+ must beat the documented-repo arm by 10 pass@1 points or 30% fewer iterations, not captured by library-only or generic-graph arms) and a defined fallback (primitives library plus lint gate at ~$15K MRR ramen profitability). Few ideas arrive with their own disconfirmation test. Second, the exit market pays for exactly what the downside case produces. Anthropic bought Bun and Stainless (reportedly >$300M), OpenAI bought Astral and Ona, Vercel bought Better Auth and Stakpak, Supabase bought Turso: model and infra vendors are buying OSS primitives with installed bases and strong teams, and a well-liked TypeScript contract library with a credible public benchmark is an acquirable asset even at sub-$100K ARR. Third, the enterprise niche, if it exists, is a $5-30M ARR governance-and-compliance category where regulators do not care which model wrote the code; compliance packs (PCI, HIPAA, SOC 2 evidence from contract coverage) are the highest-durability item in the 2030 brief at P=0.50, and they are not something a model vendor wants to own.

**Platform risk and timing.** Prisma 8 owns the word "contract" for the data layer; do not fight it. Compile to Prisma, cover actions, policies, workflows, and tests above it, and pick a name that does not contain "contract." GitHub will most likely ship the gate location (rulesets, required checks), not a TypeScript-aware contract language; be the required check that plugs into it. The window the future brief names is 12-18 months, which is precisely why a kill-gated M6 matters: it either produces the installed base and benchmark that make Warrant the partner GitHub or Prisma reach for, or it stops before the window closes on a half-built product.

**Founder focus.** This is the real objection and it should be met directly rather than argued around. Every comparable that reached revenue had full-time founders; none also ran a second startup. The kids-content survey launches this week; its results will exist by mid-November. The correct move is a dated choice, not a parallel build: run the survey, read it, and on or about December 1 pick one company. If Warrant is chosen, the founder is full-time from M1, the senior TypeScript engineer is hired in month one as planned, and the kids project goes to a holding pattern or a cofounder. A half-speed Warrant is the one configuration every brief says loses, because it arrives after CodeRabbit's pre-merge checks and GitHub's semantic rulesets have absorbed the Team-tier value.

**The decision.** Build the narrow Warrant: attach-mode library, agent-drafted human-approved contracts, generated auth-matrix tests, and a deterministic merge gate for regulated TypeScript SaaS, Apache-2.0 core, no language story in public, no `.al` grammar work, $200K cap to M6, pre-registered benchmark with the kill criteria exactly as written, and a founder who is full-time by December. If the M6 number clears, raise on it and go after the governance niche; if it does not, ship the lint gate, reach ramen profitability or take the acqui-hire, and the founder has lost six months and $200K to learn something the whole category wants to know. That is a better expected outcome than another unfunded research cycle searching for a space with less evidence than this one already has.

## Advocate position: pass

Pass. Not because the insight is wrong, but because the business that insight supports is not one a half-time founder with two people and $600K can own, and your own research says so on every page once you read it as an investor rather than as the person who wants it to be true.

Start with revenue, because it is the cleanest argument. The plan promises $150-250K ARR at month 12 and $400-700K at month 18. Your revenue brief lists twenty comparables and finds exactly one that moved at that speed: CodeRabbit, a product any GitHub repo could install in two minutes, with a $16M Series A by month 18, and it still took roughly eighteen months to reach $1M. Every declarative-backend company in the table (Encore, Wasp, Hasura pre-pivot, Prisma's ORM, ZenStack, Tessl) has either never disclosed revenue after four to seven years or is estimated under $10M after eight. Prisma, with a hundred and thirty staff and the most-loved ORM in the ecosystem, monetized only when it started hosting Postgres. Warrant hosts nothing. The brief's own base case for you is $0-60K ARR at month 12 and $100-250K at month 18. At a $28-38K monthly burn, that is a company that has spent $650K to reach a revenue level a solo consultant bills in a quarter.

The funnel math is worse than the comparables suggest. The wedge requires Prisma plus Hono or Express plus Zod plus a team willing to declare contracts. Free-to-paid conversion for developer tools runs 1-3%; applied to the M6 target of 100 weekly-active repos, that is two or three paying Team accounts, not twenty. The $20/dev price is at the category floor against CodeRabbit, Graphite, Greptile and Qodo, with first-party review from GitHub, Anthropic, OpenAI and Cognition bundled at or near zero. ## Advocate position: pass

I ran the operator's version of this question: strip Warrant to the smallest thing a solo founder could ship on nights and weekends, price it the way bootstrapped dev tools actually get paid, and check whether the arithmetic reaches $15-25K MRR by October 2027. It does not. There is a real product in here, and I describe it below, but the honest conclusion is that it is an 18-30 month grind at full attention, not a 12-month part-time ramp, and this founder does not have full attention to give.

**The smallest sellable thing.** Not the IR, not the contract tiers, not the MCP server, not the benchmark. The one piece of Warrant that needs nothing declared, and that no free reviewer does deterministically, is the pitfall checker: a GitHub App that runs on every PR of a Prisma + Hono/Express repo and fails a status check when (1) a handler that touches Prisma has no auth middleware or policy on its route, (2) a Prisma query on a tenant-scoped model has no tenant predicate, (3) a webhook handler lacks signature verification or parses the body before verifying, (4) a webhook or payment handler has no idempotency guard, (5) a Zod schema and the Prisma field it maps to disagree, (6) the diff deletes assertions, skips tests, or loosens an expect. Six rules, each with a one-line fix and actual-versus-expected in the comment. That is the "primitives library + PR lint bot" fallback the strategy document already names for month 6; the operator move is to start there and skip the $200K experiment in front of it. Everything else is dropped: the three-tier contract language, generated auth matrices, `defineAction`, `overview/find/inspect/impact`, AgentCodeBench, the Enterprise tier, SOC 2, the senior hire, the contractor.

**Price per repo, not per seat.** Agent-written code decouples PR volume from headcount, and the market has already moved: Bugbot bills per run, Greptile per credit, Vercel Agent per token. A $20 seat puts you at the category floor against first-party reviewers that cost zero. Price the bot at $39 per private repo per month or $0.75 per PR, free for public repos, no enterprise tier. At $39 the target needs roughly 450-550 paying repos. That is the number to hold against the comparables.

**Channel.** Two free assets do the selling: a one-shot web scanner ("paste a repo, get the six findings") aimed at the Lovable and Supabase incident audience, and the rule pack itself published Apache-2.0 as ESLint/Semgrep rules so it spreads without you. Distribution is the Prisma and Hono Discords, Supabase security threads, and the comments under every "our agent shipped an endpoint with no auth" post. No benchmark, no design partners, no outbound.

**Why the arithmetic still fails in 12 months.** Every bootstrapped reference point in the research is slower than this bar at full-time effort: Plausible took 324 days from first subscriber to $400 MRR with four people; Sourcery is a $10-12 seat niche after years; Inngest with a16z money took three years to $1M ARR. CodeRabbit, the fastest company in the category, installed on any repo in two minutes, had a Series A, and still needed about 18 months to reach $83K MRR. The lint bot's funnel is Prisma + Hono/Express + private repos + a team willing to pay $39 when Copilot review is bundled; dev-tool conversion runs 1-3%, so 500 paying repos needs something like 20-40K installs. Realistic curve for a part-time founder: $0-500 MRR at M6, $2-6K at M12, $15-25K at M20-30 if the rules are sharp and false positives are near zero. The only way to touch $15-20K a month inside 12 months is to sell the scanner as a fixed-price audit of vibe-coded backends at $5-10K each, two or three a month. That is revenue, not MRR, it consumes 6-10 founder days a month, and it is a job, not a company. I list it only to be complete.

**Why part-time is the specific killer, not just a handicap.** A PR bot lives or dies on false positives in its first ninety days. When the rule flags a correct webhook handler on a team's Monday morning, you have hours, not days, to ship the fix before they uninstall and say so in the Discord. A founder whose other company is launching a survey this month and reading results in November will miss those windows. Zero-to-one dev tools need daily presence; a part-time founder can run one product in maintenance mode, not one in launch mode, and both of these companies are in launch mode. The competitive window makes it worse: the future-market brief puts roughly 60% odds that GitHub, Cursor, or CodeRabbit ships the Team-tier half within 24 months, and GitHub turning rulesets into blocking semantic checks is the specific event that ends the lint bot's pricing power. A half-speed product arrives after that.

**The decision.** Pass on Warrant as a company. Pass on the IR, the contract language, the benchmark, and the $500-650K plan, and do not substitute the bootstrapped version as a side project, because a side-project PR bot in this category is the worst of both: it pulls attention from the kids-content launch and still loses to bundled reviewers on response speed. The only thing worth doing is the zero-cost option ticket: write the six rules as an Apache-2.0 ESLint/Semgrep pack over two or three weekends, publish it with a post that names the incidents, and walk away. It costs nothing, it is a reputational asset either way, and it is the cheapest possible demand test for the whole thesis: if the pack reaches 1,500 stars or draws five unsolicited "can you fail our PRs on this" requests, that is pull you did not have to invent. Revisit only if that pull appears, the kids-content company has resolved one way or the other by mid-2027 so the founder is full-time, and GitHub still only comments and never blocks. If all three hold, build the lint bot with per-repo pricing under a 6-month, $30K ceiling, not the IR. If any one fails, the pass stands.

The long-term market is real: a governance and compliance niche worth perhaps $5-30M ARR to an independent, and the vision document is right that human-approved contracts are what survives model progress. But that niche goes to whoever has an installed base and full-time hands in the next 18 months, and this founder has neither available. "Keep pivoting" should mean keep looking for a space where part-time attention is enough to win; this is not that space.
